Both apps I sell are one-time purchases. No subscription, no per-seat renewal, no "contact us for enterprise
pricing". This was not a marketing decision. It was the only model I could defend for software that runs
offline and does not need a server to work.

It also removes a lot of places to hide.

## The obvious trade

A subscription buys you predictability. Every month the revenue arrives whether or not you shipped anything,
and you can hire against it. A one-time licence means revenue stops the day after launch and only returns when
the next release is worth buying — which, for a desktop tool, usually means it is worth *upgrading*, not just
worth existing.

The consequence is uncomfortable and useful: **every release has to answer "what does this let someone do
that they could not do last month"**. Not "what did we add", not "what is on the roadmap" — what can the user
now do.

That is a better question than most roadmaps ask.

## Support is not a cost centre, it is the product

Subscriptions hide support inside the recurring fee. A one-time sale makes it explicit: the same person may
email me in year three, and no new money arrives to pay for that conversation.

Two things follow.

First, the documentation has to be good, because it is cheaper than the email. Every walkthrough on this site
exists partly because I answered the same question five times.

Second, the software has to fail loudly. A silent wrong answer costs an hour of support and, worse, damages
trust. A clear error message costs nothing and teaches the user something. I would rather refuse to run a job
than run it with a half-prepared receptor and let the user discover it in a meeting.

## The free tier is a design constraint, not a funnel

The free tier is not a teaser with a feature removed at random. It is the same program, limited where the
limit is honest:

- MolInsight handles 100 compounds per session — enough for a real series, small enough that a full screening
  campaign needs the licence.
- No time limit, no watermark on exports, no "activation required" dialog after 14 days.

The point is that someone can finish a small piece of real work, publish the figure, and never feel tricked.
If they outgrow it, they know exactly why they are paying.

The alternative — cripple the export, cripple the resolution, cripple the thing that makes the tool useful —
converts better in the short term and produces a worse product. I have watched people abandon tools for that
reason and never come back.

## What I deliberately do not sell

No team edition, no admin console, no SSO, no license server. Each of those is a real product with its own
support burden, and pretending otherwise with a checkbox would waste a buyer's afternoon.

A laboratory that needs ten seats buys ten licences and installs them. That is a smaller business, and it is
the one I can actually run well.

## The uncomfortable part

There is no growth loop here. No viral invite, no usage-based expansion, no data network effect. The only
thing that compounds is whether the software is good enough that someone mentions it in a group chat.

I have made peace with that, mostly. It means the work has to be its own argument, which is either the most
honest way to sell software or the slowest. Probably both.
