What a one-time licence forces you to get right

Both apps are one-time purchases. No subscription, no per-seat renewal, no "contact us for enterprise pricing". That was not a marketing decision. It was the only model that could be defended for software that runs offline and does not need a server to work.

It also removes a lot of places to hide.

The obvious trade

A subscription buys predictability. Revenue arrives every month whether or not anything shipped, and it can be hired against. A one-time licence means revenue stops the day after launch and returns only when the next release is worth buying — which, for a desktop tool, means it is worth upgrading, not merely worth existing.

The consequence is uncomfortable and useful: every release has to answer "what does this let someone do that they could not do last month". Not what was added, not what is on the roadmap — what the user can now do.

That is a better question than most roadmaps ask.

Support is not a cost centre, it is the product

Subscriptions hide support inside the recurring fee. A one-time sale makes it explicit: the same person may write in year three, and no new money arrives to pay for that conversation.

Two consequences.

First, documentation has to be good, because it is cheaper than the email. Every walkthrough on this site exists partly because the same question had been answered five times.

Second, software has to fail loudly. A silent wrong answer costs an hour of support and, worse, damages trust. A clear error message costs nothing and teaches the user something. Refusing to run a job is better than running it with a half-prepared receptor and letting the user discover the problem in a meeting.

The free tier is a design constraint, not a funnel

The free tier is not a teaser with a feature removed at random. It is the same program, limited where a limit is honest:

  • MolInsight handles 100 compounds per session — enough for a real series, small enough that a screening campaign needs the licence.
  • No time limit, no watermark on exports, no "activation required" dialog after 14 days.

The point is that someone can finish a small piece of real work, publish the figure, and never feel tricked. If they outgrow it, they know exactly why they are paying.

The alternative — cripple the export, cripple the resolution, cripple the thing that makes the tool useful — converts better in the short term and produces a worse product. People abandon tools for that reason and do not come back.

What is deliberately not sold

No team edition, no admin console, no SSO, no licence server. Each of those is a real product with its own support burden, and pretending otherwise with a checkbox wastes a buyer's afternoon.

A laboratory that needs ten seats buys ten licences and installs them. That is a smaller business, and it is the one that can be run well.

The uncomfortable part

There is no growth loop. No viral invite, no usage-based expansion, no data network effect. The only thing that compounds is whether the software is good enough that someone mentions it in a group chat.

That means the work has to be its own argument, which is either the most honest way to sell software or the slowest. Probably both.
