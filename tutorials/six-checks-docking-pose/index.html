Six checks before you trust a docking pose

Every docking programme will return a pose, and every pose comes with a number. The number is the part people quote; the pose is the part that has to survive a reviewer. These six checks take about five minutes per compound and catch most of the failures that make a docking figure impossible to defend.

1. Is the ligand inside the box?

Obvious, and still the most common cause of nonsense. If the box was placed by eye, confirm that the ligand volume sits inside it — a pose that only touches the edge of the search space has not really been optimised.

In MolStudio the box is drawn in the viewer, so this is a two-second visual check before the run rather than a diagnosis after it.

2. Did the protocol reproduce the reference ligand?

If the structure contains a co-crystal ligand, redock it first. Remove it, then dock it back with exactly the protocol you plan to use and check the RMSD to the crystal pose.

  • Under ~2 Å — the protocol can find the right answer on this receptor. Continue.
  • Over ~2 Å — treat every result with suspicion. Something in the preparation (protonation, missing residues, metal ions, waters) or in the box is wrong.

This is the single most valuable control in a docking study, and it costs one run.

3. Are the hydrogen bonds geometrically plausible?

Distance alone is not enough. A hydrogen bond at 3.9 Å is not a hydrogen bond; a donor pointing away from the acceptor is not one either. In the 2D interaction view, look at the listed distances and then confirm the geometry in 3D:

Interaction Typical distance What to check
Strong H-bond 2.6–3.2 Å donor–acceptor alignment
Weak / long H-bond 3.2–3.5 Å does it still point at the acceptor?
Salt bridge 2.5–4.0 Å charge state of both partners
π–π stacking 3.5–4.5 Å ring centroid distance and offset

A pose whose only interactions are 3.8 Å "hydrogen bonds" is held together by the scoring function, not by chemistry.

4. Is there any steric clash?

Look for ligand atoms that sit inside the van der Waals radius of a protein atom, and for the ligand passing through a loop. Small overlaps are absorbed by the soft scoring potential; a real clash means the pose is impossible regardless of its score.

5. Does the pose explain what you already know?

If the series has SAR, the pose should be consistent with it. A compound known to be inactive through this target should not fill the pocket perfectly. A compound that loses 100-fold when a substituent is removed should have that substituent making a contact.

Docking is at its most useful when it explains an existing measurement, not when it invents a new lead from nothing.

6. Does the ranking survive a second run?

Re-run the top compound with a different seed and higher exhaustiveness.

  • Same pose, similar score — the result is stable.
  • Different pose, similar score — the scoring function cannot distinguish them, and you should report the ambiguity rather than pick the prettier picture.
  • Same pose, very different score — the search had not converged; the first number was noise.

What the score is, and is not

The Vina affinity is a fast empirical scoring function. It is useful for ordering compounds docked into the same receptor with the same preparation. It is not a binding free energy, it does not include entropy, and it has a well-known bias toward larger, more lipophilic ligands.

Practical consequences:

  • compare within a series, never across two different receptors;
  • normalise by heavy-atom count when a series spans a wide size range;
  • treat differences under ~0.5 kcal/mol as noise;
  • report the interactions, and let the score be the ranking, not the conclusion.

A short checklist

  • [ ] Ligand fully inside the box
  • [ ] Reference ligand reproduces its crystal pose
  • [ ] Hydrogen bonds plausible in distance and angle
  • [ ] No steric clash
  • [ ] Pose consistent with the SAR you already have
  • [ ] Ranking stable across seeds

If all six pass, the pose is worth a figure. If four pass and two are ambiguous, say so — a well-described limitation is more convincing than an overconfident picture.
